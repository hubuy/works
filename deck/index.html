Case Study 01

Product Value Expression

Teaching an AI ad system to listen.

01 · The Tension

The advertiser and the AI were optimizing for different things.

AI ad delivery learns from outcomes — it's trained to maximize conversions or revenue. That works for most advertisers. It broke down for the largest ecommerce brands, who measure success at the product level: margin, inventory turn, seasonal lift, new-product traction.

The model had no way to learn those signals. So advertisers built workarounds — splitting catalogs into dozens of narrow sets, running parallel campaigns just to push specific SKUs.

“I want to bid more on high-margin, seasonal products like prom dresses — and bid less on the low performers. Right now there's no way to do that without fighting the system.” — Senior performance marketer, fashion DTC
02 · The Prior Attempt

The first version proved the demand — and exposed the wrong design.

A year before I picked this up, the team shipped PDP — Product Delivery Preference. Advertisers assigned a 0.7–1.3 multiplier to individual products. The model adjusted delivery accordingly. It worked. And it didn't.

02 · The Prior Attempt

What was broken — and what wasn't.

✕ Broken

The packaging

Per-SKU multipliers across thousands of products. Wrong tool. No semantic meaning. No reporting.

✓ Sound

The capability

Ranking infrastructure worked. Signal was being read. Delivery was responding. The system could already do the thing.

→ The reframe

Evolve the interface,
preserve the engine.

That single distinction shaped every decision that followed.

03 · Discovery

24 advertisers. 4 use cases. One pattern nobody had named.

A 4-week sprint with research and PM. The brief was deceptively simple: when you say you want to “boost a product,” what do you actually mean? Four use cases emerged — but the frequency was the real signal.

22/24

Promote & suppress by first-party signals

"Align delivery with our internal margin, stock, return-rate data. We know what's working. We just can't tell you."

20/24

Give new products a fair shot

Push products with no conversion signal yet. Advertisers will trade short-term ROAS to let new SKUs warm up.

17/24

Time-sensitive promotion

Travel, ticketing, entertainment — delivery needs to respond to price changes faster than the model's normal lag.

9/24

Match products to specific users

Marketplaces want per-user product matching. Backend-layer problem — parked for a later phase.

04 · The Insight

Advertisers weren't trying to set a number.
They were trying to express why something matters.

“A multiplier is what you give a system you don't trust.
A rule is what you give a system you do.” — Personal note, mid-project
04 · The Insight

The design thesis became a single sentence:

“I value these products X% more than the rest of my catalog.”

That sentence does three things at once — each one makes the human's signal easier for an AI system to act on:

01

Relative, not arbitrary.

“20% more” beats “1.2” — same math, infinitely more meaning.

02

By attribute, not by SKU.

“Boost women's outerwear” instead of tagging 400 products one at a time.

03

The advertiser's vocabulary.

Brand, category, stock level — not the model's number space.

Case Study 02

Unifying Product Set Intelligence

When the design problem is really a strategy problem.

01 · The Situation

Two products. One UI.
Zero path to launch.

Two parallel teams were both building toward the same advertiser surface — with conflicting models, overlapping value props, and a UI fight blocking both from shipping. Neither team could ship until someone designed the unified experience. That was my job.

01 · The Situation

Two products, one surface, no shared story.

From the advertiser's perspective, both were answering the same question — "how do I let the system pick products for me?" — with two incompatible answers. (A third workstream was adding yet another checkbox to the same surface.)

Product A · In beta · ready to launch
The Recommender (PSF)

Starts with the entire catalog. Advertiser suggests a smaller set as a hint; the system expands beyond it for better performance. Mechanic: small → big, performance-led.

Product B · In beta · also ready to launch
The Classifier (PSE)

Starts constrained to a chosen set. The system expands to relevant products via an LLM classifier. Mechanic: big → small → broadened, relevance-led.

For the business
  • • Two teams investing in overlapping solutions
  • • Conflicting roadmaps, no shared framework
  • • Both products stuck in beta, blocked by UI conflict
For advertisers
  • • Inconsistent patterns depending on product-set choice
  • • No explanation of what the platform does with their products
  • • Opt-in model — 1+ year of data showed low adoption
02 · The Reframe

The UX problem was really a value prop problem.

Two teams reconciling a UI couldn't solve it, because the conflict wasn't in the surface — it was in the strategy. Neither team could finish the sentence "an advertiser should pick this product when ___." So I stopped trying to solve the UI and started trying to map the actual problem space.

03 · Discovery

Three findings that broke the deadlock.

01 · The core insight

Experientially identical, algorithmically different.

From the advertiser's perspective, both products did the same thing: pick a set → platform expands → better performance. The only difference was how the expansion happened — and that's an engineering distinction, not a user one.

02 · The trust gap

Advertisers feared the word “expansion.”

UXR found advertisers associated expansion with losing control. They didn't know the AI could stay semantically relevant to their creative — because the UI never told them. The opacity was the problem, not the AI.

03 · The adoption paradox

Opt-in selected for the wrong advertisers.

A year of opt-in data showed only the most sophisticated advertisers enabled expansion. The ones who needed it most never found the toggle. The default was the wrong lever.

“Show my products, and if you can do better, go ahead — just don't make it weird.” — What almost every advertiser actually wants · the line that became the design thesis
04 · The Insight

One spectrum. Four modes.

How much control do you want vs. how much flexibility do you give the platform?

← More platform flexibility More advertiser control →
MODE 1

Show all products

Full Liquidity

MODE 2 · fka PSF

Prioritize a set

Your set + top performers from anywhere

MODE 3 · fka PSE

Prioritize + related

Your set + AI-matched relevant items

MODE 4

Limit which products

Only what you selected

The conflict dissolved — they weren't competing, they were neighbors on a spectrum nobody had drawn yet.

05 · The Shoe Retailer Test

If a framework is right, one advertiser proves it.

A shoe retailer. 5,000-SKU catalog. “Summer Sandals” campaign with 50 products selected.

MODE 1

Show all 5,000

By predicted conversion. May surface winter boots.

MODE 2

Boost 50 + high-performers

Your set as the seed. Recommender pulls in whatever converts.

MODE 3

Boost 50 + relevant items

LLM reads creative. Adds espadrilles, not winter boots.

MODE 4

Only the 50 sandals

No expansion. Upsell offers 11% lower CPR if they switch.

“The difference isn't how many products get added — it's which ones, and why.” — The line that resolved the engineering debate

06 · The Design · Principles

Three design decisions worth defending.

01

Default to performance — transparently.

Shifted the adoption model from opt-in to opt-out. The dropdown makes the default visible, so it feels informed, not imposed.

02

Preserve existing settings.

No surprise changes to live campaigns. Migration logic maps each existing state to the nearest dropdown equivalent. No one has to redo their setup.

03

Sticky opt-out.

An explicit “No expansion” choice persists even as defaults change. Trust requires respecting deliberate decisions.

Opt-in to opt-out changed the adoption trajectory more than any UI improvement could.

07 · What's Shipping

Pragmatic phasing — three steps to the ideal world.

Each phase validates an assumption the next one builds on. Nothing has to be unlearned.

Phase 1 · H1 2026

Pre-checked checkbox

Existing UI stays intact. Expansion ships as default-on opt-out.

Validates: Does expansion lift performance, and do advertisers uncheck it?

Phase 2 · H2 2026

Preference selector

“What” (product set) and “how” (expansion) become separate controls.

Validates: Do advertisers pick the right mode when given the choice?

Ideal world

Unified 4-mode dropdown

One decision point. Four self-describing modes. Full progressive disclosure.

Ships once Phase 2 data confirms the model holds.

Apr 30, 2026 milestone: Phase 1 QE launched with both expansion modes in a single cohort — validates the unified mechanic without fragmenting the signal.

Liquidity · Reflection

What this taught me about AI product design.

01

Designing for AI is choice architecture.

Helping users choose between AI behaviors — not just turning AI on or off.

02

Defaults are the most powerful lever.

Opt-in to opt-out changed the trajectory more than any UI improvement could — but only when the default is transparent.

03

Specificity is clarity.

The Shoe Retailer Test communicated in 10 seconds what a three-page spec couldn't.

PVE
